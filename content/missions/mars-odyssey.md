## Summary
The oldest working spacecraft at Mars: launched on 7 April 2001 and in orbit since 24 October 2001, it marks 25 years in orbit in 2026 ([NASA — Odyssey](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/odyssey/)). It maps the composition of the surface and relays data from the rovers.

## Now
Operating: in October 2026 it was described as the oldest of the working Mars orbiters ([The Conversation / New Space Economy, 19 October 2026](https://newspaceeconomy.ca/2026/10/19/nasas-mars-orbiter-fleet-is-getting-old-heres-how-that-could-affect-future-exploration/)). Fuel is the main limit. The spacecraft has no fuel gauge; a recalculation in 2023 gave about 4 kg of hydrazine, enough "at least until the end of 2025" ([Astronomy, 16 March 2023](https://www.astronomy.com/space-exploration/nasas-oldest-mars-orbiter-just-got-a-new-lease-on-life/)). In December 2025 Ars Technica wrote that the fuel would probably run out within the next couple of years ([Ars Technica](https://arstechnica.com/space/2025/12/nasa-just-lost-contact-with-a-mars-orbiter-and-will-soon-lose-another-one/)). There is no official end date.

Since the loss of MAVEN, Odyssey and MRO carry most of the relay traffic, and scientists say their future depends on NASA's budget ([Science News, 5 June 2026](https://www.sciencenews.org/article/nasa-maven-mars-atmosphere-dead)).
